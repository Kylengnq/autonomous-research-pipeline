# Research proposal outline

Source: *How to write a research proposal*, Bayes Business School Executive PhD
programme, 2024. Reproduced here as the structural standard the pipeline's G1
proposals follow, so no skill depends on a file outside the workspace.

A proposal "sets out the central issues or questions that you intend to address",
outlines the general area and the current state of knowledge, and **demonstrates
the originality of the proposed research**. It is also evidence that the author
"can communicate complex ideas concisely and critically".

## The seven required sections, in order

### 1. A working title
Concise and descriptive; informative and, if possible, catchy. A compelling title
gets the reader's interest and predisposes them favourably. Revisable later.

### 2. Background
A brief overview of the proposed area of study that **identifies the discipline
or disciplines within which it falls**. The purpose is to fix the *setting* —
the topic and the discipline together. The same problem read by different
disciplines yields different research questions, so the discipline has to be
named rather than implied.

### 3. Research questions
Stated explicitly. **Not too general or vague.** A good question identifies a
relevant problem *and* is answerable within the timeframe available. The question
often follows from the setting chosen in section 2.

The evaluator's question is: **in what way is your question new?** Answering it
requires familiarity with what is already known, so novelty is argued against
named prior work rather than asserted.

### 4. Literature review
Cohesive and focused rather than comprehensive — it is a proposal. It must show:

- familiarity with the proposed area of study;
- awareness of current debates and issues;
- familiarity with the key studies;
- **an identified gap**: that the proposed questions have not been studied before.

Six named failure modes to avoid:

1. failing to identify the reference literature for the broad area;
2. failing to cite seminal papers;
3. no awareness of recent developments;
4. repetition of information;
5. over-reliance on non-academic sources;
6. (implied throughout) citing without showing relevance to the research area.

### 5. Data and proposed methodology
Must show awareness of both the data and the methodological tools.

On data: what type is needed; whether it is available; whether it must be
collected (surveys, interviews) or already exists; which databases hold it.

On method: which approach — qualitative, quantitative, or a combination — and
**why those methods fit these questions**. The guidance is explicit that "saying
your research is quantitative or qualitative is not sufficient". Partial thinking
is acceptable and should be shown as current thinking rather than hidden.

### 6. Implications
**Who benefits.** Three routes, and a proposal may take more than one: a
practical contribution to industry or an employer; a direct impact on government
policy; a contribution to academic knowledge.

### 7. List of references
Every journal article, book and industry publication used in the proposal.
